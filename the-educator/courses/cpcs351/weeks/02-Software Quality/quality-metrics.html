Quality Measurements & Metrics

“Good quality” is an opinion until we decide what evidence to observe. Metrics turn software characteristics into measurable signals that engineers can compare, question, and improve.

“Do not ask for one magic quality number. Ask which part of the software you are trying to understand.”

Measurement → Metric

From measurement to metric

First we measure software attributes objectively, then standardize useful measurements as metrics, and use quality metrics to assess software quality.

Software Measurement

An objective, quantitative assessment of a software attribute.

→

Requirements Metrics

The Requirements Quality Compass

Before measuring code, we can measure the quality of what we asked the team to build. Four essential properties are un-ambiguity, completeness, correctness, and consistency.

GOOD
REQUIREMENT
Un-ambiguityOne clear interpretation.
CompletenessNothing essential missing.
CorrectnessRepresents the intended need.
ConsistencyNo contradictions.
Requirements Measures

Number of user interviews and number of “shall” statements are examples of requirements metrics.

Important interpretation

More requirements do not automatically mean better requirements. The four quality properties help us ask whether the set is actually useful.

Example · Defect Density

Why normalization changes the story

Service A

8 defects found in 4 KLOC.

8 ÷ 4 = 2 defects/KLOC
↔

Service B

4 defects found in 1 KLOC.

4 ÷ 1 = 4 defects/KLOC

Service B has fewer total defects, but its defect density is twice as high. A raw count and a normalized metric can tell different stories.

Illustrated Story

From “looks good” to measurable evidence

Panel 1

Everything looks clean

The team says the system “looks good.” No one can explain what “good” means.

Panel 2

Choose the lens

Are we worried about requirements, architecture, code, system behavior, or class design?

Panel 3

Measure

The team collects consistency, fan-out, defect density, complexity, and class-coupling evidence.

Panel 4

Reason, don’t worship numbers

The metrics reveal where to investigate. Engineers still interpret the evidence in context.

Mentor Insight

A metric is a signal, not a verdict. High fan-out, deep inheritance, or high complexity may indicate risk—but context determines whether that risk matters.

Key Takeaway

Software measurements objectively quantify attributes. Metrics standardize those measurements across requirements, design, implementation, system, and object-oriented software, using measures such as requirement consistency, fan-in/fan-out, cohesion, coupling, defects/KLOC, cyclomatic complexity, inheritance depth, class coupling, response for a class, and lack of cohesion in methods.
