Software Quality Assurance

Quality assurance is not a person waiting at the end of development. It is a set of activities that protects both the software product and the process used to create it.

“The engineering question is not only ‘Does it work?’ It is also ‘Was it built correctly, does it satisfy the right need, and what did failure cost us?’”

Original Lecture Concept

What is Software Quality Assurance?

The lecture defines SQA through its purpose: ensuring conformance to established requirements and quality standards.

Software Quality Assurance (SQA) is a set of activities to ensure that the software product and/or process conforms to established requirements and quality standards.

Four Activities Named in the Lecture

How SQA looks at software

The original material explicitly identifies four major activities. They overlap, but they answer different questions.

01

Verification

Are we building the product right? Check whether an implementation conforms to its specification.

02

Validation

Are we building the right product? Check whether what we model and build corresponds to real customer needs.

03

Technical Reviews

People examine software work products to discover defects, inconsistencies, or non-compliance before those problems travel further.

04

Testing

Execute software in an attempt to uncover program errors and observe its behavior.

Cost of Quality

Quality has a cost. Failure has a cost too.

The lecture separates quality cost into two families. This is a crucial engineering trade-off: spending resources to prevent failures versus paying for failures after they occur.

Prevention Costs

  • Implement an SQA framework — once.
  • Perform SQA activities — ongoing.
  • Monitor and improve the framework — ongoing.
  • Provide needed equipment, machines, and tools.
VERSUS

Failure Costs

  • Analyze and remove failures.
  • Lost productivity — developer and customer.
  • Liability, lawsuits, and increased insurance premiums.
  • Damage to the company's reputation.
Engineering Trade-off

More prevention can mean less failure cost

Software engineering considers the cost required to accomplish a goal. Quality therefore involves a quality–cost trade-off, not an unlimited pursuit of perfection.

Example · University Registration System

One small requirement, two very different costs

1 · Requirement

“A student must satisfy prerequisites before registering.” A reviewer notices that concurrent prerequisites were never defined.

2 · Prevent Early

The SRS is clarified before design and coding. The team changes a few lines of requirements.

3 · Escape Late

The ambiguity reaches production. Students register incorrectly and schedules, billing, and academic records are affected.

4 · Engineering Lesson

Early SQA did not merely “find a typo.” It prevented downstream rework and operational damage.

Mentor Warning

Do not confuse SQA with testing. Testing is one SQA activity named in the lecture. SQA is broader: it also includes verification, validation, and technical reviews, and it concerns both product and process.

Key Takeaway

SQA is a set of activities used to make the software product and/or process conform to requirements and quality standards. It includes verification, validation, technical reviews, and testing. Investing in prevention costs money—but failures can cost far more, especially when defects survive until later stages.

Think Like a Software Engineer

A defect costs 20 minutes to clarify in the requirements meeting. What could the same defect cost after deployment?

Think beyond developer time: customer productivity, data correction, support, reputation, liability, and the cost of changing interconnected software.
