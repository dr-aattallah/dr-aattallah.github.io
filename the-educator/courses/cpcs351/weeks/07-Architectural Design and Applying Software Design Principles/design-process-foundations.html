The Design Process & Foundational Principles

Design is problem solving under functional, non-functional, budget, and quality constraints.

12of 14
Learning focus

Cover the design process, design aspects, bad-design symptoms, divide-and-conquer, design for change, separation of concerns, and information hiding.

  • Design finds and describes a way to implement functional requirements while respecting non-functional requirements, budget constraints, and general principles of good quality.
  • Design aspects include architecture design, class design, user-interface design, algorithm design, and protocol design.
  • Architecture design determines subsystem/component division, connections, interactions, and interfaces.
  • Bad design examples include low functional cohesion: unrelated use cases assigned to one subsystem or unrelated methods grouped in one class; another bad-design type is high coupling.
  • Software design principles are widely accepted guiding rules; applying them correctly can significantly improve software quality.
  • Divide and conquer: split large problems into smaller parts so people can work in parallel, engineers can specialize, components are easier to understand, and parts can be changed/replaced with less impact.
  • A software system can be divided into clients and servers, subsystems, packages, classes, and methods.
  • Design for change: anticipate business changes, defect fixes, hardware/platform/environment changes, government/industry changes, and improvements in performance, reliability, efficiency, security, and user-friendliness.
  • Separation of concerns: focus on one aspect at a time. UML examples: class diagrams focus on structure, sequence diagrams on object interactions, and use-case diagrams on use cases/context.
  • Separation of concerns applies at two levels: the overall design process and the design of individual components.
  • GUI components should focus on presentation, database components on storage/retrieval, and each use case on one business process.
  • Information hiding: shield implementation details to reduce change impact. Private data members and stable class interfaces help; operations such as first() and next() can hide underlying data representation.
