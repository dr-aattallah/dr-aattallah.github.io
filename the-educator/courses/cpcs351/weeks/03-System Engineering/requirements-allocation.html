Requirements Allocation

A system requirement belongs to the whole system first. Allocation answers the next question: which subsystem—or combination of subsystems—will be responsible for delivering it?

“Requirements do not disappear after architecture. They must land somewhere—and someone must own the capability.”

Decomposition Example

R1 becomes a requirement tree

A broad requirement such as “check in and transport luggage” is too large to assign directly. It is decomposed into smaller capabilities that reveal where responsibility belongs.

R1 · ABHS shall check in and transport luggage to departure gates and baggage claim areas according to passenger destinations.
↓
R1.1 · Check-In

ABHS shall allow airline agents to check in luggage.

R1.2 · Transport

ABHS shall transport luggage to destinations within the airport.

R1.3 · Control

ABHS shall control transportation of luggage within and between terminals.

↓ R1.2 decomposes further ↓
R1.2.1Check-in areas → departure gates.
R1.2.2Arrival gates → baggage claim areas.
R1.2.3Arrival gates → departure gates for transfers.
R1.2.4Within terminals using conveyors.
R1.2.5Between terminals using DCVs on high-speed tracks.

Performance Allocation

R4 becomes a capacity budget

The system-level requirement to serve 20,000 passengers per day must be translated into capacities that individual parts can actually design for and test.

R4 · ABHS shall be able to serve 20,000 passengers per day. System performance is distributed into measurable subsystem responsibilities.

Closer to Students

University Registration System: the same idea

Suppose the system requirement is: “The university shall allow 5,000 students to register during the first hour of registration.” Allocation turns that system goal into responsibilities.

SYSTEM REQUIREMENT · 5,000 STUDENTS IN THE FIRST HOUR
Infrastructure

Servers, database capacity, network, and load balancing must handle the expected traffic.

Software

Registration logic, prerequisite checks, seat updates, and APIs must process requests efficiently.

Human / Operations

Support staff, escalation procedures, and operational monitoring must handle exceptions and incidents.

Hardware scans tags, software stores data, and agents search for bags—but responsibilities do not line up.

The requirement is decomposed and primary responsibility is assigned while interfaces are documented.

Panel 4

Trace the capability

The team can now follow the original requirement into hardware, software, and human responsibilities.

Mentor Insight

Allocation is not merely distributing sentences among teams. It is preserving the system-level capability while deciding which parts are responsible for delivering measurable pieces of it.

Key Takeaway

Requirements Allocation decomposes system requirements and assigns responsibility to subsystems. R1 shows functional decomposition of baggage check-in, transport, and control; R4 shows how a system-level performance target becomes measurable capacity requirements for check-in areas, agents, conveyor hardware, and control software.

Think Like a Software Engineer

A requirement depends equally on hardware, software, and human procedures. Who owns it?

Decide whether it needs one primary owner, shared ownership, or a higher-level system owner—and explain how you would prevent the requirement from falling between team boundaries.
