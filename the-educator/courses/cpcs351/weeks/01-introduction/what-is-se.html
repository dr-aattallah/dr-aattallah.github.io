What Is Software Engineering?

Software engineering is not simply programming. It applies engineering processes and methods to the creation and evolution of software—with measurable goals for productivity, quality, cost, and delivery time.

From the Original Lecture

The core definition

Software engineering as a discipline focuses on the research, education, and application of engineering processes and methods to significantly increase software productivity (P) and quality (Q) while reducing software costs (C) and time to market (T).

The lecture summarizes these four goals as Software PQCT.

The Big Idea

Software PQCT

Engineering is about achieving a useful balance. A software team is not successful merely because it produces code; it must produce valuable software effectively and at an acceptable level of quality, cost, and delivery speed.

Software Engineering
↑ P

Productivity

Increase the useful software value a team can deliver with its available effort and resources.

↑ Q

Quality

Build software that satisfies requirements and demonstrates the required reliability, security, usability, maintainability, and other relevant qualities.

↓ C

Cost

Control the effort, people, infrastructure, maintenance, rework, and other costs required to create and operate the software.

↓ T

Time to Market

Reduce the time required to move from an identified need to useful software delivered to its intended users.

The objective is not to maximize one metric blindly. Software engineering uses disciplined decisions to improve the overall outcome.

Engineering Mindset

Programming is part of a larger process

Writing code is essential, but software engineering begins before coding and continues after the first version is deployed.

Modern Example

A university registration system

One system · four engineering goals

“It works” is not enough.

Imagine a university is preparing its online registration system for thousands of students. The team could technically implement the required features, yet the project may still fail if it ignores PQCT.

ProductivityCan the team add and modify registration rules efficiently without repeatedly rebuilding the same functionality?
QualityDoes the system correctly enforce prerequisites, protect student information, remain available under heavy demand, and prevent incorrect registrations?
CostCan the institution develop, operate, support, and maintain the system within reasonable resources?
Time to MarketWill the required capabilities be ready before registration opens, when students actually need them?
Important: PQCT involves trade-offs

Trying to reduce delivery time too aggressively may increase defects or future maintenance cost. Pursuing extremely high quality can also require more time and resources. Software engineering helps teams make these trade-offs deliberately rather than accidentally.

Think Like a Software Engineer

A team delivers early and under budget—but the system frequently fails. Was the project successful?

Evaluate the result using all four PQCT dimensions. Then consider the opposite case: a nearly perfect system that arrives one year after the business opportunity has disappeared.
