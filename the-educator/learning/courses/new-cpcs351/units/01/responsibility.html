Responsibilities and consequences

Sommerville highlights four practical areas of professional responsibility: confidentiality, competence, intellectual property rights, and computer misuse. These are useful because they turn “ethics” from an abstract slogan into decisions engineers encounter in ordinary work.

ConfidentialityProtect entrusted information—even when no convenient technical control forces you to.
CompetenceDo not misrepresent expertise or knowingly accept responsibility you cannot perform safely.
Intellectual PropertyRespect ownership, licensing, copyright, patents and organizational assets.
Computer MisuseTechnical capability is not permission. Access and power must be used legitimately.

Ethics becomes difficult when duties conflict

Real dilemmas are rarely “good versus evil.” Confidentiality may conflict with public safety. Delivery pressure may conflict with adequate validation. Loyalty to a team may conflict with reporting a serious defect. Professional judgment requires identifying affected parties, possible harms, obligations, evidence, alternatives and escalation paths—not simply choosing the fastest technical action.

◇ ExampleETHICAL DECISION SCENARIO

“Ship it. We’ll fix the audit log next sprint.”

RoomNow is scheduled for launch tomorrow. The team discovers that administrator changes to room permissions are not reliably logged. The product manager says no student can see the log anyway, so the defect is invisible. What should the engineer do?

Technical lensWhat can fail? Can unauthorized changes be reconstructed?
Stakeholder lensWho could be harmed by untraceable permission changes?
Professional lensWhat evidence and escalation are appropriate before release?

CampusCare · compare separate decision dimensions

Use “Functional Needs” for the misprinted heading in the original illustration. More importantly, its four options overlap. A custom-built solution can use open-source components and run in the cloud; these are not four mutually exclusive alternatives.

Separate the dimensions before comparing solutions
DimensionCompareEvidence to collect
AcquisitionBuild a tailored solution / adapt an existing platformRequired capabilities, integration effort, skills, change cost
Licensing and supportOpen-source / proprietary componentsLicense obligations, maintenance ownership, support and security response
Hosting and operationUniversity-managed / externally hosted serviceApproved data handling, availability and recovery commitments, total cost

The pictured cloud choice is a hypothesis, not a proven recommendation. It is defensible only if institutional approval, integration tests, recovery evidence and a cost comparison support it. If a mandatory privacy constraint is not met, speed does not make that option acceptable.

Notifications: SMS and push are possible delivery channels, not guarantees that a person received a message. Compare reach, user consent, delivery evidence and an appropriate fallback. Keep health details out of notification previews.

✎ ExerciseENGINEERING JUDGMENT

Do not answer with “yes/no.” Build the decision.

  1. State the risk and uncertainty.
  2. Identify affected stakeholders and possible harm.
  3. Propose at least two responses.
  4. Identify evidence that would change your decision.
  5. Justify the recommendation.
◇ Example

See how the reasoning improves

A team wants to upload real CampusCare appointment records to a public debugging service without established approval.

Weak response

“Remove names and upload them.”

Stronger response — and why

Removing names alone does not establish permission or prevent identification. Use synthetic examples or an authorized environment under an applicable policy, and verify the data-handling decision.

✎ Exercise

Your turn · two minutes

You are uncertain whether sharing a diagnostic record is permitted. State a safer immediate response and the uncertainty that must be resolved.

Ungraded self-study practice. Your text is lost on reload; copy it before leaving, or answer on paper.

Compare your reasoning, then revise

Pause the unapproved sharing, use synthetic data where suitable, and check the relevant authorization and policy with the responsible institutional role. Do not infer approval from convenience or from the absence of names.

Revise: add the stakeholder, uncertainty or observable evidence missing from your first response. Different justified answers are possible.

Carry forward: Clarify authorization and possible harm before sharing data; convenience does not establish permission.
