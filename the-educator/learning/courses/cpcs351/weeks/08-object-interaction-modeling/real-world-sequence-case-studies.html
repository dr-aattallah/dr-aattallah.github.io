Real-World Sequence Diagram Case Studies

Follow how objects collaborate over time in HungerStation and Snapchat. Read the scenario first, then trace messages, decisions, activations, and object creation.

15of 15
Learning objectives

Identify actors, boundary/control/entity lifelines, synchronous messages, dashed replies, execution specifications, and UML combined fragments (alt, opt, loop). Explain how object interactions realize a use-case scenario, and distinguish successful, alternative, and conditional flows.

Before you begin

How to read these diagrams

Time flows from top to bottom. Participants are arranged horizontally; messages connect lifelines. Solid-line arrows typically show calls; dashed arrows show returns. An open arrowhead may indicate an asynchronous message. Thin rectangles indicate execution/activation. alt separates mutually exclusive guarded paths, opt marks conditional behavior, and loop indicates repetition.

Modeling distinction: A class diagram shows structural relationships; a sequence diagram illustrates one interaction scenario. Controllers and user-interface boundaries may be introduced during design even when they are absent from the domain model.

Case Study 01 · Food Delivery

HungerStation — Food Ordering and Delivery

Scenario: Ahmed chooses a restaurant, adds two burgers to his cart, selects a delivery address, places an order, chooses online payment or cash on delivery, and follows the order through restaurant acceptance and delivery.

1. Browse & selectThe mobile app displays restaurants and a menu; Ahmed adds food items to his cart.
2. Checkout & createThe controller validates checkout and creates an Order.
3. Pay & prepareOnline payment is authorized by the payment gateway; cash on delivery is the alternative. The restaurant then accepts or rejects the order.
4. Deliver & notifyThe courier collects and delivers the order; Ahmed receives delivery updates.

Select the diagram to open its full-resolution image. On narrow screens, scroll horizontally to follow the lifelines.

Trace the interaction

  1. Browse and build the cart: Ahmed uses the app; the controller returns restaurant data and the cart records each chosen item inside a loop.
  2. Validate checkout: the controller requests the cart total. The alt fragment distinguishes valid from invalid checkout.
  3. Create and pay: after confirmation, the controller creates Order. Payment follows the online/COD alternative; payment failure does not lead to restaurant fulfillment.
  4. Accept and deliver: the restaurant accepts or rejects the request. On acceptance, the delivery service collects the food and later confirms delivery.

What to observe

  • Actor → boundary → control: Ahmed interacts with the app, which delegates application logic to an order controller.
  • Object creation: The Order lifeline begins at the <<create>> message; Cart already exists.
  • Guarded paths: Payment approved/declined and restaurant accepted/rejected are alternative outcomes, not consecutive steps.
  • Optional/repeated behavior: Adding cart items repeats in a loop; placing an order requires a valid checkout.
  • State transitions: An accepted order progresses through preparation, pickup, transit and delivery.
Think: Why does the controller create an Order only after checkout validation? What happens after a declined payment?
Case Study 02 · Social Messaging

Snapchat — Send Snap and Publish Story

Scenario: Sara captures and optionally edits a photo, then attempts to send it to Ahmed. In a separate interaction, Sara publishes a Story. Ahmed may open either item only when the relevant access rules allow it.

1. Capture & editSara takes a photo and may add stickers or a caption.
2. Create & sendThe controller checks permission before creating Snap and the recipient-specific delivery record.
3. Open & recordAhmed opens an authorized Snap; its recipient-specific viewing status is recorded.
4. Publish & viewSara publishes a Story; access depends on privacy settings and whether the Story remains active.

On a small screen, scroll horizontally inside the diagram. Open the full-resolution version to zoom into individual messages.

Trace the interaction

  1. Prepare: Sara captures a photo and may edit it using an opt fragment.
  2. Send: the app delegates to SnapController, which checks permissions before creating Snap and SnapDelivery.
  3. Deliver and open: delivery and viewing are separate events. Ahmed's access is checked before the media is returned.
  4. Publish a Story: Sara starts a separate publication interaction. Story viewing requires both authorization and an active, unexpired Story.
Important: Part B is a separate use-case interaction. Publishing a Story does not depend on Ahmed opening the Snap in Part A.

What to observe

  • Separate actors: Sara sends content while Ahmed receives and views it.
  • Optional editing: The opt fragment runs only if Sara edits the Snap.
  • Recipient authorization: Communication and Story visibility depend on permission checks.
  • Delivery versus viewing: A delivered Snap is not necessarily viewed. The SnapDelivery record stores recipient-specific delivery and view status.
  • Story lifecycle: The Story's availability depends on its expiration time; viewing it does not itself cause expiration.
Think: Sara sends one Snap to Ahmed and Khalid, who open it at different times. Why is a separate SnapDelivery record required for each recipient?

Reading tip: The diagrams are teaching models of selected interactions, not exhaustive specifications of either commercial application. Do not infer undocumented internal architectures from the participant names. A return message indicates a response; a new event (such as delivery confirmation) can occur later, outside the original synchronous call.
Exam takeaway

For each message, identify the sender, receiver, and responsibility. Check that calls and returns align with execution bars; object lifelines start at creation. Use alt for mutually exclusive outcomes, opt for conditional behavior, and loop for repetition. Never continue a failed path as if it succeeded.
