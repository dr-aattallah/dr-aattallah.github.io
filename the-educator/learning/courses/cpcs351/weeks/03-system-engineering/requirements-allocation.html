Topic 03 · Lesson 05

Requirements Allocation

Requirements allocation turns whole-system obligations into clear, measurable responsibilities for subsystems, components, software, hardware and human procedures.

5of 8 pages
Learning focus

Explain progressive requirement refinement, decompose ABHS requirements across useful levels of detail, and trace lower-level requirements back to the original system requirement.

What is requirements allocation?

From “what the system must do” to “what each part must do”

Allocation distributes or refines a high-level requirement into more specific obligations assigned to the subsystem, component, interface, software, hardware or human element that will satisfy it.

1 · System intentWhat must the whole system achieve?
2 · AllocateWhich part owns the responsibility?
3 · RefineWhat exactly must that part achieve?
4 · VerifyCan we measure and test it?
Level 1

System

Whole-system obligation: what the complete system must achieve.

Level 2

Subsystem

Responsibility assigned to a major subsystem or organizational part.

Level 3

Component / Mechanism

Responsibility refined into specific hardware, software, interface or human mechanisms.

Level 4

Detailed / Design

Specific, measurable and testable constraints used to guide implementation and verification.

Study model: these four levels are a useful way to visualize progressive refinement. They are not a mandatory four-level standard prescribed by the lecture; real projects may use more or fewer levels.

Official lecture example · ABHS

One hierarchy, increasing specificity

System Requirements

R1ABHS shall check in and transport luggage to departure gates and baggage claim areas according to passenger destinations.
R2ABHS shall allow airline agents to inquire about luggage status and locate luggage.
R3ABHS shall check all baggage and detect prohibited items.
R4ABHS shall serve 20,000 passengers per day.

Subsystem-Level Refinement

R1.1ABHS shall allow airline agents to check in luggage.
R1.2ABHS shall transport luggage to destinations within the airport.
R1.3ABHS shall control transportation of luggage within and between terminals.
R4.xR4 must be distributed into capacity and performance targets across responsible subsystems.

Component/Mechanism Refinement

R1.2.1Transport luggage from check-in areas to departure gates.
R1.2.2Transport luggage from arrival gates to baggage claim areas.
R1.2.3Transport luggage from arrival gates to departure gates for transfer passengers.
R1.2.4Transport luggage within a terminal using conveyors.
R1.2.5Transport luggage between terminals using DCVs running on high-speed tracks.

Detailed Quantitative Requirements

R4.1Each check-in area shall handle 1,150 pieces of check-in luggage per day.
R4.2Each check-in agent shall check in an average of three passengers per minute.
R4.3Each conveyor hardware shall scan and transport 500 check-in pieces of luggage per hour.
R4.4ABHS control software shall process 2,300 check-in bags per day and 1,000 bar code scan requests per hour.
Two allocation patterns in the same example

Functional decomposition vs. performance allocation

R1 · Functional refinement

R1 begins as an end-to-end transport obligation. It is refined into R1.1, R1.2 and R1.3, then R1.2 is further decomposed into specific transport paths and mechanisms R1.2.1–R1.2.5.

R4 · Quantitative allocation

R4 begins as a whole-system capacity target: 20,000 passengers/day. That target is distributed into measurable capacities for check-in areas, agents, conveyor hardware and control software.

Sequential refinement · top down

How the requirement travels through the design

1 · High-level requirementDefine the whole-system obligation.
→
2 · AllocateAssign responsibility to one or more subsystems.
→
3 · RefineSplit broad obligations into detailed ones.
→
4 · QuantifyAdd measurable capacity, timing or quality targets.
→
5 · Trace & testEnsure every lower-level requirement supports a parent requirement.
Key idea

Lower-level requirements are not independent inventions. Together they must fully support the intent of their parent requirement.

Transfer example · Smart Hospital

Allocate a medication-safety requirement

System requirement: “The system shall verify patient identity before medication release.” A plausible allocation might assign identity capture to the scanner hardware, identity matching to verification software, and an override/escalation responsibility to the nurse procedure. The important point is traceability: every allocated responsibility must support the original patient-safety obligation.

Apply it · Is this allocation complete?

Scenario: The team allocates patient-ID scanning to hardware and matching to software, but nobody owns what happens when the scanner cannot read a wristband.

Reveal reasoning: No. The parent requirement is not fully covered because the failure/exception path has no accountable subsystem or human procedure. Allocation must cover the complete intent, not only the happy path.

Misconception: “Allocation means copying the same requirement into every subsystem specification.”

No. Allocation assigns or decomposes responsibility. Each lower-level requirement must contribute to satisfying its parent, while traceability keeps the chain visible.

Exam lens: Be able to distinguish system, subsystem, component and detailed views as useful refinement levels; show how R1 is decomposed and how R4 is quantitatively allocated; and explain why the lower-level set must collectively satisfy the original requirement.
Key takeaway

Architecture defines the parts. Requirements allocation makes each part accountable, measurable and traceable to the system-level need.
