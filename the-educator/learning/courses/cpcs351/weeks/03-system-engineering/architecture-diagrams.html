Architectural Design Diagrams

Different diagrams answer different engineering questions. Learn what each view is for, what its notation emphasizes, and how to read the official examples.

6of 8 pages
Learning Focus

Distinguish block, UML component, SysML block-definition, SysML internal-block, and data/material-flow views, and choose the view that best communicates the required relationship.

Before the diagrams

One system can need several views

Block diagram

Best for a simple high-level picture of major system parts and connections.

UML component

Best for software/component structure, ports, interfaces and communication paths.

SysML BDD / IBD

Best for multidisciplinary system structure, composition, ports and internal flows.

Data/material flow

Best for showing what information or physical material moves through functions and entities.

1 · Block Diagram

Use it for the big structural picture

A block diagram gives a fast, technology-neutral overview of the major subsystems and the flows or control relationships between them. It is ideal when the first question is simply: What are the major parts, and how do they interact?

High-level structure
How to read it

Identify the major blocks first. Then follow arrows and labels such as luggage, data and instructions to understand the interactions.

What this ABHS example reveals

The Terminal and High-Speed Tracks subsystems move luggage, while ABHS Control Software coordinates through data/instruction flows and interacts with Flight Information.

2 · UML Component Diagram

Use it when software/component interfaces matter

A UML component diagram emphasizes components, ports, interfaces, dependencies and communication paths. It is especially useful when software and hardware components are organized into subsystems and the engineering question is about who communicates with whom.

Components & interfaces
How to read it

Start with subsystem boundaries, then identify components inside them. Small square ports mark interaction points; connectors show communication paths; labels such as events, instructions and call data tell you what travels across them.

Why this view is useful

It makes interface responsibility explicit—valuable when separate teams must integrate independently developed components without ambiguity.

3 · SysML Block Definition Diagram (BDD)

Use it to define the system’s structural composition

A SysML BDD shows what blocks exist and how the whole is composed from parts. It is a strong fit for multidisciplinary systems because a block may represent hardware, software, people, facilities or other system elements.

Composition / whole–part
How to read it

The top block is the whole. The branches identify constituent blocks. In this example the black diamond represents composition—a strong whole–part relationship.

BDD vs. Block Diagram

Both show structure, but the BDD uses SysML semantics to express formal block relationships rather than only informal boxes and arrows.

4 · SysML Internal Block Diagram (IBD)

Use it to see inside a block and follow its ports and flows

An IBD takes a block defined structurally and opens it up to show internal parts, ports and item/material/information flows. It answers a deeper question than the BDD: How do the parts inside this subsystem actually interact?

Internal structure & flow
How to read it

Follow the nested boundary first, then locate ports on block edges. Trace luggage, IDs, bar codes and instructions through the ports to see how human, hardware and software components collaborate.

BDD vs. IBD

BDD: “What blocks make up the system?” IBD: “How do the parts inside one block connect and exchange flows?”

5 · Data Flow Diagram with Material Flows

Use it when both information and physical items move through the process

A conventional data-flow view emphasizes information. In system engineering, the model can be extended to show material flow as well—useful when the system transforms or transports physical items such as luggage, publications, packages or products.

Information + material movement
How to read it

Use the legend: processes/functions transform work, external entities sit outside the system boundary, internal entities/data stores hold material or information, and different arrow styles distinguish information flow from material flow.

Why this matters in SE

System behavior often includes both cyber and physical movement. A baggage system, for example, moves data about a bag and the bag itself—those are different flows that must remain synchronized.

Study comparison

Which diagram should you choose?

Need a quick map of major subsystems? Block diagram. Need component interfaces? UML component diagram. Need formal multidisciplinary composition? SysML BDD. Need internal ports and nested flows? SysML IBD. Need to follow information and physical material through functions? Data/material-flow diagram.

Apply it · Choose the view

Scenario A: You must show hospital managers the major medication-delivery subsystems without overwhelming them with notation. Best choice: Block diagram.

Scenario B: Engineers must inspect how scanner hardware, verification software and nurse-workflow interfaces connect inside the Patient Verification subsystem. Best choice: SysML IBD.

Scenario C: You must distinguish the physical movement of medication from electronic prescription data. Best choice: Data/material-flow diagram.

Misconception: “The most detailed diagram is always the best diagram.”

No. A diagram is an abstraction chosen for a question. Extra detail can obscure the relationship you need to reason about.

Key takeaway

Do not ask “Which diagram is the correct one?” Ask “Which view makes this engineering relationship easiest to see?”
