Topic 03 · Lesson 04

System Architectural Design

Architecture decomposes the system into a hierarchy of cohesive, loosely coupled subsystems whose responsibilities and interfaces can be engineered separately and later integrated.

4of 8 pages
Learning Focus

Explain why systems are decomposed, apply the six decomposition guidelines from the lecture, and distinguish functional partitioning from HW/SW/Human partitioning.

Core lecture

What architectural design must accomplish

System architectural design has three connected jobs: decompose the system, allocate system requirements, and visualize the resulting architecture. The decomposition should produce functionally cohesive, loosely coupled subsystems, support reuse of COTS components, and make system responsibilities explicit.

Architecture vs. allocation

Do not confuse the boxes with their obligations

Architecture
Decides what major parts/subsystems exist, where boundaries are drawn, and how those parts relate through interfaces.
Requirements Allocation
Decides what each part is responsible for satisfying, and refines whole-system requirements into accountable lower-level obligations.

Memory rule: Architecture answers “What parts do we have?” Allocation answers “What must each part do?”

Six decomposition guidelines

A good partition is more than a neat drawing

1 · Separate engineering teams

The result should allow different teams to develop subsystems with manageable responsibilities.

2 · Facilitate COTS use

Boundaries should make it practical to adopt commercial off-the-shelf components where suitable.

3 · Partition requirements

Subsystems should partition—or nearly partition—the system requirements so ownership is clear.

4 · Well-defined functionality

Each subsystem needs a coherent purpose rather than an arbitrary collection of tasks.

5 · Relative independence

Subsystems should be loosely coupled enough that teams can reason about and change them without constant ripple effects.

6 · Easy integration

Interfaces and responsibilities should support later assembly and interoperability testing.

Two official partitioning strategies

Different boundaries expose different engineering views

Partition according to major functionality

Group components that cooperate to deliver a major system function. Each functional subsystem may itself contain HW, SW and human elements.

Check-in subsystem → HW + SW + human
Transport subsystem → HW + SW + human
Security/scanning subsystem → HW + SW + human

Use when: you want each subsystem to own an end-to-end business or operational function.

Partition according to HW, SW & Human subsystems

Group the system first by engineering discipline, then decompose within each discipline.

Hardware subsystem → devices, conveyors, scanners
Software subsystem → control, data, interfaces
Human subsystem → operators, procedures, staffing

Use when: discipline-specific engineering ownership and specialization dominate the organization of work.

Worked example

ABHS architecture

A functional view can decompose ABHS into check-in, transport, scanning, routing/monitoring and human operations. A discipline-oriented view can instead emphasize hardware, software and human subsystems. Neither is universally “correct”; the useful architecture is the one that produces coherent responsibilities, manageable interfaces, requirement coverage and feasible integration.

Transfer example · Smart Hospital

Two valid architectural views of the same hospital system

Functional partition: Prescription Validation, Dispensing, Delivery, Patient Verification, and Clinical Monitoring—each may contain hardware, software and humans. Discipline partition: Hardware, Software and Human/Clinical Procedure subsystems. The choice changes team boundaries and interfaces, but the system mission remains the same.

Apply it · Which partitioning strategy is this?

Scenario: A hospital architecture has three top-level subsystems named “Medication Ordering,” “Medication Delivery,” and “Patient Verification.” Each contains software, devices and human roles.

Reveal reasoning: This is partitioning according to major functionality because each subsystem owns an end-to-end operational capability rather than one engineering discipline.

Architecture → allocation

Why the next lesson matters

Architecture identifies the parts and interfaces. Requirements allocation then makes those parts accountable by assigning or refining whole-system obligations into subsystem and component requirements. A subsystem without allocated responsibility is only a box; allocation turns it into an engineering commitment.

Misconception: “A good architecture minimizes the number of subsystems.”

The objective is not the fewest boxes. The objective is meaningful cohesion, manageable coupling, clear responsibility, feasible integration and complete requirement coverage.

Key takeaway

Good decomposition creates subsystem boundaries that teams can build independently enough to work efficiently—but integrate reliably enough to satisfy the whole-system mission.
