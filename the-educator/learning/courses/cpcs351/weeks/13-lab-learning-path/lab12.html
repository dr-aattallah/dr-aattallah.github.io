Lab 12 · Design Quality

Design Principles & Prototyping

Control complexity by decomposing software into purposeful modules, design for reuse and controlled interfaces, hide implementation decisions, and use prototypes to reduce uncertainty when requirements are incomplete.

DecompositionModularityReuseInformation HidingPrototyping
Learning focusApply fundamental software-design concepts and use modeling/prototyping tools to improve a proposed solution.
Core tensionMore modules can improve manageability, but excessive fragmentation increases integration effort.
Quality directionLower coupling, controlled interfaces, easier change, effective testing and reusable components.

Decomposition

Large monolithic designs are difficult to build and change. Decomposition applies divide-and-conquer: select a piece of the problem, identify components using an appropriate design paradigm, describe their interactions, then repeat until the design is sufficiently manageable.

Modularity

Partition software into uniquely named, addressable modules that can be developed with a degree of independence and later integrated. Good modularization supports planning, change, testing, debugging and maintenance.

Software reuse

Design components so useful capabilities can be reused rather than rebuilt. Reuse is intended to increase productivity and reduce repeated effort.

Prototyping

When requirements are incomplete, a quick mock-up lets users and developers explore the proposed system, refine requirements and reduce the risk of building a solution that does not match customer needs.

Information hiding

Modules should expose only what collaborating modules need through controlled interfaces while hiding internal data structures and processing decisions. This encourages lower coupling, limits ripple effects of change and supports higher-quality maintenance.
