Why Software Engineering?

Scale, complexity, dependence, consequences, and teamwork make ad-hoc development increasingly risky as software systems grow.

4of 11 pages
Before You StartWhen does “just build it” stop being a reasonable software strategy?

Think about what changes when one developer becomes ten teams and one script becomes a service people depend on.

Core lecture content Clarification & study aids
Learning Focus

Justify the need for software engineering by connecting system scale, complexity, team size, and failure consequences to specific engineering practices.

Core Lecture

Why Ad-Hoc Development Stops Scaling

  • Software is used across nearly every sector, and systems continue to grow in size and complexity.
  • Large systems require many engineers to work together; individual programming practices do not scale automatically to organizational development.
  • Failures can affect business, safety, security, reputation, service continuity, and cost.

Worked Example

Airline Reservation System

A change to seat allocation can affect mobile apps, web services, airport systems, databases, payments, customer notifications, and operational procedures. The engineering problem is therefore coordination and controlled change as much as coding.

Remember

As scale and consequences grow, coordination, explicit models, verification, and disciplined change become essential.

Common Misconception

“Software engineering is only needed for huge or safety-critical systems.” The amount of engineering should fit the risk and complexity, but even modest shared systems benefit from clear requirements, change control, testing, and coordination.
