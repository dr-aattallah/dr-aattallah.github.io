Topic 02 · Lesson 05

Inspections, Walkthroughs & Peer Review

Compare the major verification and validation techniques by purpose, execution, participants, structure and the artifacts they can examine.

5of 9 pages
HookFive techniques can all contribute to quality—but they do not look, feel, or operate the same way.

Use the visual anchors below to remember the difference before memorizing definitions.

Learning Focus

Distinguish desk checking, testing, walkthrough, peer review, and inspection by execution, participants, structure, purpose and applicable artifacts.

Purpose Shared by Peer-Review Processes

What Reviews Are Trying to Achieve

  • Determine whether the work product satisfies its specifications.
  • Detect deviations from standards and expected practice.
  • Identify defects, inconsistencies and suspicious anomalies.
  • Provide constructive suggestions for improvement where appropriate.

Important: review and inspection are not limited to source code. Static representations can be examined before executable software exists.

Five Techniques — Fast Comparison

Static · Individual

Desk Checking

One person reads the code or artifact alone. It is informal and less effective than walkthrough or inspection.

Dynamic · Execute

Testing

Execute the software and observe behavior. Testing attempts to uncover program errors.

Static · Guided

Walkthrough

The developer manually walks through the work, often using simple test data, while participants ask questions.

Static · Peers

Peer Review

Peers evaluate independently, answer review questions, then discuss findings with the developer.

Static · Checklist

Inspection

A structured defect-detection examination using criteria, common-error checklists, standards and consistency rules.

Static vs. Dynamic: The Fast Separation

Static

Desk checking, walkthrough, peer review, inspection. Analyze a representation without executing the product.

Dynamic

Testing. Execute the product and observe what it does.

Program Inspection: What Counts as a Defect?

Examples include logical errors, suspicious anomalies such as an uninitialized variable, and non-compliance with standards. The inspector’s goal is detection; correction is a later responsibility. Inspection does not require execution, can be used before implementation, and can examine requirements, design, code, test data and other system representations.

Walkthrough vs. Inspection

Walkthrough

Uses simple test data, manual simulation, step-by-step reading aloud, and discussion that stimulates questions and doubt.

Inspection

Uses a defined list of criteria: common errors, standards, and consistency rules. The team systematically examines the product against those criteria.

Peer Review Procedure

The reviewers may have one to two weeks to evaluate the product. Results can vary with reviewer knowledge, experience, background, and criticality.

Present a product overview and assign material to reviewers.
Reviewers evaluate independently and answer the review questions.
At the review meeting, reviewers present comments; the developer answers questions and clarifies doubts.
The developer fixes problems and produces a summary of solutions.
Reviewers check the changes; a second meeting may be needed.
Worked Example

One Requirement, Three Review Styles

Requirement: “The system shall lock an account after five failed logins.” A desk check is one reviewer reading it alone. In a walkthrough, the author guides peers through login scenarios. In an inspection, reviewers systematically check the requirement against defined criteria such as ambiguity, consistency and standards. Later, testing executes failed logins dynamically.

Choose the Technique

Your goal is consistent detection of known coding-standard violations across a team. Walkthrough or inspection?

Self-Feedback: Inspection is the stronger fit because it explicitly uses criteria/checklists for common errors, standards, and consistency rules. A walkthrough is better suited to manual simulation and discussion around the developer’s work.
