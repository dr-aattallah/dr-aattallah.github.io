Software Quality & SQA

Build a complete mental model of software quality assurance before studying its techniques.

Can software pass every test you wrote and still be a low-quality product? Yes—quality is broader than “no observed bugs.”
Learning Outcome · CLO #2

The complete quality story

Define software quality and explain quality assurance in the software process; distinguish verification, validation, reviews, testing, metrics and quality attributes in context.

Quality Goals→Cost→V&V→Reviews→Metrics→Attributes
Must Know

What Is Software Quality Assurance?

SQA is a set of activities used to ensure that the software product and/or process conforms to established requirements and quality standards.

VerificationAre we building the product right?
ValidationAre we building the right product?
Technical ReviewsExamine artifacts without waiting for execution.
TestingExecute software to uncover errors.
Evidence Model

Do not collapse the terms into one bucket

Static evidenceRequirements, design and code can be examined without executing software. Reviews and inspections can find defects early.
Dynamic evidenceTesting requires execution and compares observed behavior with expected behavior or needs.
Verification questionDoes the work product conform to specification, standards or preceding artifacts?
Validation questionDoes the product/model satisfy the intended real-world need?
Misconception: SQA ≠ testing. Testing is one SQA activity among reviews, V&V, standards, measurements and process-oriented quality work.
Apply · Online Examination System

One requirement, several kinds of evidence

“After time expires, answers shall be submitted automatically.” A reviewer checks the design; a tester runs the timer; students judge whether the behavior supports the intended workflow.
Design checking = static verification. Executing the timer = dynamic testing and verification evidence. Judging fitness for the real examination workflow = validation-oriented evidence.

Cost of Quality

Quality spending is an economic engineering decision, not optional overhead.

Would you rather pay to prevent a defect today—or pay for it after thousands of customers depend on the system?
Official Lecture Content

Two sides of the Cost of Quality

Prevention / Assurance Cost
  • Implement SQA framework — once
  • Perform SQA activities — ongoing
  • Monitor/improve framework — ongoing
  • Equipment, machines and tools
Failure Cost
  • Analyze and remove failures
  • Lost developer/customer productivity
  • Liability, lawsuits, insurance premiums
  • Damage to company reputation
Spending nothing on prevention does not make quality free—it shifts spending toward failure.
Visual Anchor

The later a defect escapes, the more expensive it becomes

Requirements
≈ 1→Design→Coding→Development Test→System Test→Field Operation
up to ≈ 1000

The exact multiplier is less important than the engineering lesson: find problems early.

Saving all quality effort for system testing ignores defect propagation into design, code, tests, documentation, deployment and customer operations.

Verification vs. Validation

Separate conformance to specification from correspondence to real customer needs.

A system can perfectly implement the wrong requirement. Is that success?
Core Distinction

Two different quality questions

Verification — “Are we building the product right?”A lower-level abstraction should correctly implement the higher-level specification. Example: design verification checks design against requirements.
Validation — “Are we building the right product?”Check correspondence between model/product and real-world customer need. The lecture describes validation as seeking to refute a model; functional testing is given as a validation technique.
Customer Need→Requirements→Design→Code→Running System
Diagnose the Failure

Hospital reminder example

The system exactly implements “send reminders 24 hours before the visit,” but users need 48 hours because of transport constraints.
The implementation may verify correctly against the written requirement, yet the requirement fails validation against the real-world need. Verification is not simply “developer testing,” and validation is not simply “customer testing.”

Quality evidence follows artifacts from requirements through operation.

Critical Concept

Question ≠ evidence-gathering technique

ValidationCorrespondence to real-world need and intended customer use.
StaticReview, walkthrough, inspection, peer review and desk checking without execution.

Topic 02 · Lesson 05

Inspections, Walkthroughs & Peer Review

Five techniques contribute to quality, but differ in execution, participants, structure, purpose and applicable artifacts.

Visual Anchor

Software Verification & Validation Techniques

Desk checking · Testing · Walkthrough · Peer Review · Inspection

Shared Review Purpose

What reviews are trying to achieve

  • Determine whether the work product satisfies specifications.
  • Detect deviations from standards and expected practice.
  • Identify defects, inconsistencies and suspicious anomalies.
  • Provide constructive suggestions for improvement where appropriate.
Reviews are not limited to source code: Requirements/SRS, Design, Source Code, Test Data and other static artifacts can all be examined.
Five Techniques · Fast Comparison

Static and dynamic quality work

Desk Checking · StaticOne person reads the artifact alone; informal and less effective than walkthrough or inspection.
Testing · DynamicExecute software and observe behavior to uncover program errors.
Walkthrough · StaticDeveloper manually walks through work, often with simple test data; participants ask questions.
Peer Review · StaticPeers evaluate independently, answer questions, then discuss findings with developer.
Inspection · StaticStructured defect detection using criteria, error checklists, standards and consistency rules.
Inspection vs. Walkthrough

Purpose and procedure matter

WalkthroughSimple test data, manual simulation, step-by-step reading aloud, discussion that stimulates questions and doubt.
InspectionDefined criteria: common errors, standards and consistency rules; systematic examination. Inspector detects; correction comes later.

Inspection can detect logical errors, uninitialized variables, standards violations and other anomalies without executing software.

Peer Review Procedure

A structured feedback cycle

1 · AssignPresent product overview and assign material.
2 · Independent ReviewReviewers evaluate and answer review questions.
3 · MeetingReviewers present comments; developer clarifies doubts.
4 · Fix & SummarizeDeveloper fixes problems and summarizes solutions.
5 · RecheckReviewers check changes; second meeting may be needed.

Reviewers may have 1–2 weeks. Results vary with knowledge, experience, background and criticality.

Worked Example

“Lock account after five failed logins.”

Desk CheckOne reviewer reads it alone.
WalkthroughAuthor guides peers through login scenarios.
InspectionCheck ambiguity, consistency and standards systematically.
TestingExecute failed logins dynamically.

Quality Measurements & Metrics

Turn quality claims into quantitative evidence across requirements, design, implementation and OO software.

Visual Anchor

Measurement vs. Metric

Software measurements are objective quantitative assessments of attributes. Metrics are standard measurements. Software quality metrics are standard measurements of software quality.
Complete Lecture Taxonomy

Four families of quality metrics

R · RequirementsUnambiguity · Completeness · Correctness · Consistency
D · DesignFan-In · Fan-Out · Cohesion · Coupling · Modularity · Module Design Complexity · Design Complexity
C · ImplementationDefects/KLOC · documentation/KLOC · comment lines/LOC · cyclomatic complexity
S · System / OOWeighted methods/class · inheritance depth · children · class coupling · response/class · lack of cohesion in methods
Requirements Metrics

Complete list and interpretation

UnambiguitySingle clear interpretation.
CompletenessRequired information and behavior are specified.
CorrectnessRequirements accurately represent what is required.
ConsistencyRequirements do not conflict.
“Respond quickly” → “For the specified workload, 95% of responses shall complete within 2 seconds.” The second is easier to verify and measure.
Design Metrics

Definitions from the lecture

Fan-In / Fan-OutModules calling a module / modules called by it.
Cohesion / CouplingRelevance to core functionality / data dependencies among modules.
ModularityHigh cohesion + low coupling → high modularity.
ComplexityModule Design Complexity: integration tests with subordinate modules. Design Complexity: subtrees with module M as root.
Implementation + OO Metrics

Complete lecture coverage

ImplementationDefects/KLOC; pages documentation/KLOC; comment lines/LOC; cyclomatic complexity = independent control-flow paths (McCabe, 1979).
Object-Oriented / SystemWeighted methods/class; depth of inheritance; number of children; coupling between classes; response for a class; lack of cohesion in methods.
Metrics are signals for investigation, not replacements for engineering judgment. Equal LOC can hide very different control-flow complexity.

Software Quality Attributes

High quality is a multidimensional profile, not a single score.

A fast system can be hard to maintain; a reliable system can still be difficult to use.
Five Attributes

The lecture's core quality vocabulary

UsabilityLearn quickly and get the job done easily.
EfficiencyAvoid wasting CPU time and memory.
ReliabilityDo what is required without failing.
MaintainabilityBe easy to change.
ReusabilityReuse parts in other projects.

Their relative importance depends on context.

Scenario Map

See each attribute in a real situation

Usability · RegistrationStudent registers without training or unnecessary steps.
Efficiency · SearchSame correct result with much less CPU and memory.
Reliability · PaymentRequired transaction repeatedly succeeds under expected conditions.
Maintainability · PolicyFocused module changes instead of unrelated rewrites.
Reusability · AuthenticationOne component serves registration, advising and alumni apps.
“If it works, it is high quality” is incomplete: these dimensions describe different aspects of quality.

Short-Term vs. Long-Term Quality

Judge software against today's needs and tomorrow's change and scale.

Two Horizons

Quality changes with the time horizon

Short-Term Quality
  • Meet immediate customer needs?
  • Efficient for today's data volume?
Long-Term Quality
  • Maintainability
  • Support future customer needs
  • Scalability for larger data volumes
“Good enough today” may be rational when future risks are considered. Overengineering wastes effort; ignoring likely evolution creates expensive technical debt.
Look Beyond Today

2,000 students now; 50,000 later

A registration system handles 2,000 students perfectly but requires a rewrite for 50,000. Is it low quality?
Short-term quality may be acceptable while long-term scalability is weak. The judgment depends on whether growth to 50,000 is a realistic future requirement.

Software Engineering vs. Computer Science

Connect quality engineering to broader engineering trade-offs.

Why might an engineer knowingly choose a solution that is not mathematically optimal?
Lecture Framing

Computer Science → Software Engineering

Computer Science
  • Pursue optimal solutions
  • Cost not primary consideration
  • Programming in the small
  • Technical issues
  • Tame problems
Software Engineering
  • “Good enough” can be enough
  • Cost matters — PQCT
  • Programming in the large
  • All issues and aspects
  • Wicked problems
  • Builds on CS and other disciplines
Topic 02 Review

Key takeaways

  • SQA ensures product/process conformance to requirements and standards.
  • Quality cost includes prevention/assurance and failure; late defects cost more.
  • Verification = work product vs specification; Validation = product/model vs real need.
  • Static techniques examine artifacts; dynamic testing executes software.
  • Inspection, walkthrough, desk checking and peer review differ in rigor/process/purpose.
  • Metrics quantify evidence but require context and interpretation.
  • Quality attributes: usability, efficiency, reliability, maintainability, reusability.
  • Quality includes short- and long-term dimensions, scalability and future change.
