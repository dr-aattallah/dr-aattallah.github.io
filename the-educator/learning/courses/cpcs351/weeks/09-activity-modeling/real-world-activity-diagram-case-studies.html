Real-World Activity Diagram Case Studies

Trace one object's lifecycle through states, events, guarded transitions, and outcomes using HungerStation and Snapchat.

15of 15 pages
Learning objectives

Read UML activity diagrams using initial and final nodes, action nodes, decision/merge nodes, guarded flows, loops, fork/join synchronization, and activity partitions (swimlanes). Explain who performs each action and how alternative paths affect the workflow.

Behavioral modeling · Workflow

How does work move across participants?

Unlike a sequence diagram, which emphasizes message exchange, or a state machine, which describes the states of one object, an activity diagram describes the flow of actions, decisions, and responsibilities in a process.

UML notation: The filled circle marks the initial node; rounded rectangles are actions; diamonds represent decisions or merges; arrows show control flow; thick bars indicate fork/join synchronization; swimlanes assign responsibility; and the bullseye indicates an activity final node. Branch labels such as [Yes] and [No] act as guards.
Case Study 01 · Food Delivery

HungerStation — Food Ordering and Delivery

Scenario: Ahmed chooses a restaurant, adds two burgers to his cart, confirms an address, optionally applies a discount, and places the order. The payment gateway, restaurant, and delivery service cooperate to fulfill the request. This diagram models the end-to-end workflow, not the lifecycle of one Order object.

1 · Shopping and checkoutBrowse menu, build cart, validate address, apply an optional discount.
2 · Payment and restaurantAuthorize online payment or choose cash on delivery; restaurant accepts or rejects.
3 · Parallel fulfillmentFood preparation and courier assignment proceed concurrently before pickup and delivery.

Use the full-resolution view to inspect individual guards and swimlane handoffs. On mobile, zoom in to read the actions.

Follow the workflow

  1. Start and actions: Ahmed opens the app, selects food, and proceeds to checkout.
  2. Loops: The customer can add more items or correct an invalid address.
  3. Decisions: Optional discounts, payment success/failure, and restaurant acceptance create guarded branches.
  4. Swimlanes: Customer, Mobile App, Order System, Payment Gateway, Restaurant, and Delivery Service identify responsibility.
  5. Fork and join: Preparing the food and assigning a courier can happen in parallel; the workflow synchronizes before pickup.
  6. Final outcomes: The order can be completed, cancelled after payment failure, or rejected by the restaurant.

Case Study 02 · Social Messaging

Snapchat — Send Snap and Publish Story

Scenario: Sara captures and optionally edits a photo, sends it to Ahmed, and may also publish it as a Story. The system checks communication permissions, transmits the Snap, handles retries, and records delivery or viewing. Story access depends on audience settings and expiration.

1 · Capture and editTake a photo, optionally add a sticker or caption, and choose Ahmed.
2 · Send and receiveValidate privacy rules, send, retry if needed, deliver, and optionally open the Snap.
3 · Story publicationOptionally publish a Story and verify audience access before viewing.
Open full-resolution diagram ↗Save PNG

Follow the workflow

  1. Optional branch: Sara can send an unedited Snap or add stickers and captions first.
  2. Guarded permission: A SnapDelivery record is created only when communication is permitted.
  3. Retry and waiting: Transmission and delivery may require additional attempts when unavailable.
  4. Different actions: Sending, delivering, and viewing are distinct workflow steps.
  5. Responsibility: Sara acts in her swimlane; the Snapchat System validates delivery; Ahmed opens content; the Story Service manages audience rules.
  6. Story decisions: Publication is optional, and viewing depends on authorization and availability.

Connect the three views: Sequence diagrams answer who communicates with whom? The state machine diagrams answer what state does the object enter? These activity diagrams answer what work happens next, under which conditions, and by whom?
Exam takeaway

Start with actions and responsibility. Add decision/merge nodes for alternatives, loops for repetition, and fork/join bars only for genuinely concurrent work. Keep the diagram focused on workflow—not object lifecycles or message order.
